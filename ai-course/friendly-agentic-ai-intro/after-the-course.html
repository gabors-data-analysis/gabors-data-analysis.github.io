After the Course

You are home, with a working agent, referee notes and a better prompt. What to do next, in order: finish what is open, make the rules and the skills yours, and run a project of your own

⌂ Overview 1 Setup 2 First steps 3 A paper in 50 minutes A Assignment 4 After the course 5 Go further 6 AI in science

What do we do in this part?

The session is over. You have a working agent, a small project of your own from Part 2, and the World Cup project with its assignment. What should you do with them?

This part is a path through the next few weeks, and it stays with the case study. First you finish what is open. Then you turn what you learned from the World Cup paper into rules and skills of your own. At the end you run the same kind of project on a question from your own field, and review it.

Follow the steps in order. The times are our estimates.

When Step Time
The first week 4.1 Finish what is open about four hours in all
The second week 4.2 Grow your AGENTS.md half an hour
4.3 Make the skills yours about an hour
4.4 Keep a history of every change ten minutes, or an hour with Git
When you have a free afternoon 4.5 Run your prompt on your own question an afternoon
4.6 Review your own project about an hour
NoteWhat you will have at the end

4.1 Finish what is open

The first week

Two things are open after the session.

Your first project. In class we got as far as Step 2.4. Two steps of Part 2 are left.

  • Step 2.5: the memo, your review, the revision. Do this one first. A draft, comments on paper, a revision: it is the loop you will use most in your own work.
  • Step 2.6: a first task with data. This is a separate assignment, on a new question. If you don’t work with data, you may skip it.

Open first-project in the Code tab and start a new session. The agent doesn’t remember the class, so begin with: “Read status.md and tell me where we left off.”

The Assignment. You read the World Cup project as a referee would, and you rewrite its prompt with skills.

You are done when you have docs/memo_v2.docx in first-project, and your referee notes and prompt_v2.md in the World Cup project folder.

4.2 Grow your AGENTS.md

The second week

A skill is a routine you call when you need it. A rule in AGENTS.md holds always. Some of what you found as a referee belongs there.

Open your referee notes from the assignment next to your AGENTS.md. For each problem, ask: would a standing rule have prevented it? Add two or three rules, in your own words. For example:

- Every reference has a DOI or a link that you opened. If you cannot open it, mark the reference as unverified.
- Before you estimate anything, show me the design and wait for my OK.

From here on the rule for growing the file is simple. When the agent makes the same mistake twice, add one line. After a month it will fit how you work better than any template.

When your projects involve data, these lines are worth adding:

- Put checks in the code (row counts, duplicate IDs, merge matches, value ranges, missing values). After each run, save all checks with pass/fail in output/checks.md. If a check fails, stop and tell me.
- Any choice I did not specify goes into DECISIONS.md, marked needs-review.

And keep the file short. A long file is read less carefully, by the agent too. Once a year, ask: “Which lines in AGENTS.md are out of date, or say the same thing twice?”

4.3 Make the skills yours

The second week

The eight skills you have are short on purpose. They are a beginning, and they were written by economists. Now develop them further.

Change one. Pick the skill that is closest to your own work. Each skill ends with ideas under Develop it further. Ask the agent to propose changes, and decide yourself:

Read .claude/skills/referee/SKILL.md. I work in [your field], mostly with [your kind of data or sources]. Propose three additions that a referee in my field would expect. Show me the changes and wait for my OK before you save anything. Keep the skill under two pages.

Then try it: run the changed skill on the World Cup project, and see whether the report got better.

Write one. The best candidates are routines you have repeated two or three times, such as the review loop of Step 2.5. You don’t have to write it yourself. At the end of a session where the routine worked well, ask:

We just went through a routine that worked: reading my comments from a PDF, sorting them into language and content, fixing the language, and asking me about the content one at a time. Write it as a skill in .claude/skills/pdf-comments/SKILL.md, following the format of the read-pdf skill. Keep it under one page.

Then read it, as you would read instructions you give to a new research assistant.

Look at what others wrote. Scott Cunningham’s MixtapeTools has the fuller originals of five of our skills. Anthropic keeps a public collection at github.com/anthropics/skills, and Neal Caren has a set for sociological research (see the useful links). Read a skill before you install it, whoever wrote it: it is a set of instructions that your agent will follow.

You are done when one skill has a check from your own field, and /pdf-comments shows up when you type / in a new session.

4.4 Keep a history of every change

The second week

In the next step the agent will work for a long time and write many files. Before that, make sure you can go back. There are two ways, a simple one and a precise one.

The simple one: a cloud folder. If your project folder is in Dropbox, OneDrive or Google Drive (Step 1.2), you can already go back in time: each file has a version history. If it is not there yet, move ai-projects there now. For most of us this is enough.

The precise one: version control, usually Git with GitHub. Git keeps a history of your project folder: each commit saves a snapshot of every file, with a one-line note on what changed. GitHub keeps a private copy of that history online. It adds two things. First, you see what changed across sessions and days, line by line. Second, every mistake can be undone by going back to the last version that worked. This makes it cheap to let the agent try things.

The price is about an hour of setup and one habit: commit after each step that works. The agent does most of the typing, and GitHub Desktop shows the history without the terminal. For setup, see Version control with Git and GitHub. For daily use, see Git for working with agents.

TipLet the agent run Git, with you deciding each step (click to open)

Once Git is set up, you can run it entirely through the agent. You read and decide, and the agent does the typing.

1. Review before you commit. Ask for a summary first, and commit only when it matches what you wanted:

Summarize, in plain language, what changed since the last commit: which files, and what changed in each. Propose a one-line commit message. Do not commit yet.

If the summary lists something you didn’t ask for, ask about it before you go on.

2. Commit and push. When the summary is right:

Commit with that message, then push to GitHub.

3. Undo. If a step turns out wrong, even days later:

Revert the last commit, keeping the history. First tell me what this will undo, and wait for my OK.

A revert adds a new commit that undoes an old one, so the record of what happened stays complete. You can also ask about the past: “What changed since Monday, and why?”

If you set up Git, add two lines to AGENTS.md:

- Commit only when I ask. Never push to GitHub without asking.
- Never rewrite or delete Git history.

4.5 Run your prompt on your own question

When you have a free afternoon

Now do what the demo did, on a question you know well. That is the point of choosing your own field: you can judge the result.

Choose the question. It should be one that open data can answer: official statistics, a public survey, a published replication package. Keep confidential data out of it (Step 1.2).

Prepare the folder. Make a new folder in ai-projects. Copy your AGENTS.md and the .claude folder with your skills into it. Put the three or four papers you know best on the question into docs/readings/.

Adapt your prompt. Start from prompt_v2.md, the prompt you wrote in the assignment. Change three things.

  1. The question and the audience. Yours, in one or two sentences, for readers in your field.
  2. The literature. In the demo the agent was told to ignore the literature. Here it should use the papers you gave it, as in Part 2: “The papers in docs/readings/ are the ones I know. Use the read-pdf skill, and cite them with pages.”
  3. The size. A first run can be smaller: 8 to 10 pages, three to five charts and tables.

Keep the stop after the design. The design is the decision that matters most, and it is yours.

Run it. Start in Plan mode and read the plan, as in Step 2.3. For the run itself, Manual mode means a click for every file. Accept edits or Auto fits better here (Step 2.1), as long as the folder holds nothing confidential. Then watch the four things of Part 3: the plan, the choices, the checks and the files.

A long task uses up your usage limit faster than anything in Part 2. If the agent pauses at the limit, nothing is lost: the files are in the folder, and status.md says where the work stands. For the choice of model on long tasks, see Which model should you use?

It worked if the folder has a paper, the scripts that made every number in it, and a DECISIONS.md you can read. It doesn’t have to be a good paper yet.

The first attempt won’t be a paper you can submit. But you will see, on a question you know well, what the agent does well and where it needs you.

4.6 Review your own project

When you have a free afternoon

In Step 2.5 a fresh session reviewed the memo. For a project with data and code, the review goes further: the reviewer reruns everything from scratch. Start a new session and send:

You are reviewing work that someone else did in this folder.
Read AGENTS.md, DECISIONS.md and the scripts in code/.
Rerun all scripts from scratch, in order. Do the outputs match the ones already saved?
Then list anything that looks wrong, untested or undocumented, most serious first. Do not change any files.

This is what the referee skill does, in more steps. If you installed the skills, type /referee in the new session instead, and then /ref-check and /paper-check.

Read the review as you would read a referee report: some points matter, some are noise. Take the serious ones back to the agent, one at a time, each in a new session.

Then be the referee yourself. Go through the eight questions of the Assignment, this time on your own paper. You know this literature and these data better than the agent does, so you will find more than you found in the World Cup paper.

Finally, write down how the work was made: which model, on which date, and what you checked by hand. You will need it when you describe the use of AI in a paper. See Decision logs, reviewing agent work, and disclosing AI use in the knowledge base.

You are done when you can say, for your own draft, what you said for the World Cup paper: what it claims, what you checked, and what would have to change before you put your name on it.

Where are you now?

You have run a project in small steps (Part 2), watched one run from a single prompt (Part 3), refereed it and improved its prompt (the Assignment), and run one of your own with a review (this part). That is the whole routine. From here on it is practice, on your own questions.

Part 5 is different. It has no steps. It is broader advice for the time when the agent is part of your work: other things to use it for, and how to choose your tools.
