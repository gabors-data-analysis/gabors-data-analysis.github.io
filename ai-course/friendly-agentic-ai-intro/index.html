Friendly Introduction to Agentic AI for Social Scientists

A mini course: from a chatbot that polishes your text to an AI agent that works with your files

Published

November 10, 2026

A mini course in four parts

Friendly Introduction to Agentic AI for Social Scientists

From a chatbot that polishes your text to an AI agent that works with your files: your readings, your Word drafts, your data

⌂ Overview 1 Setup 2 First steps 3 Staying in charge 4 A paper in 50 minutes

Who is this course for?

You are a social scientist: a sociologist, a political scientist, an anthropologist, an economist. You have used ChatGPT or Claude in a browser, mostly to improve a text or as a better search engine. You haven’t used it for the actual work of your field, and you may never have written a line of code. This course is for you. Seniority doesn’t matter.

We hear two reactions from colleagues.

“This is for programmers.” It isn’t. You won’t type a single command. You give instructions in plain language, as you would to a research assistant. Even the technical setup is done by the agent, which explains each step before it takes it.

“I don’t need this.” Maybe. The course lets you find out on your own material, for the price of an afternoon and one month of subscription. You remain the author: nothing changes in your files without your approval.

We use one tool: Claude Code, in the Claude desktop app, on Windows, Mac or Linux. Similar agents exist, and what you learn here carries over. But when learning, one tool is enough.

What is an AI agent?

Let’s start with what you know. A chatbot is a conversation in a browser. You paste in a text or upload a file, it answers, and you copy the answer back into your document. It knows only what you gave it in that conversation.

An agent is the same AI model with three additions. First, it has access to one folder on your computer, which you choose. Second, it can act there: open files, write and run small programs, save results. Third, it works in a loop: it takes a step, looks at the result and decides on the next one, until the task is done or it needs you.

The loop makes the difference. Suppose you ask for a chart of turnout by age group from survey.csv. A chatbot needs the file uploaded and may guess what the columns mean. The agent does the following:

  1. it opens the file and sees that age is called age_cat, not age;
  2. it writes a short program and runs it;
  3. it hits an error because some ages are coded “refused”, and asks you how to handle them;
  4. it runs the program again, opens the chart to check it, and saves it in output/.

You see every step, and at first it asks your permission before each one.

It helps to think of the agent as a very fast research assistant sitting at your computer. It has read a lot, but it is new to your project, and it can be wrong with confidence. Hence your job shifts from doing each step to instructing the agent and checking its work. Checking matters most.

What can it do for a social scientist?

Here are a few tasks that fit an agent well. None of them needs you to write code.

A pile of readings. Thirty PDFs in a folder become a table: research question, data, method and main finding, each with a page number you can check.

A draft in Word. The agent comments on the argument, or edits a copy, and you review each edit as a tracked change.

Interviews and open-ended answers. A first pass over transcripts: recurring themes, each with word-for-word quotes and where they come from. Interpretation stays with you.

A survey or a public dataset. Download, clean, tabulate and chart, with every step saved in a script you can rerun.

The dull parts. Reformatting references for another journal, checking that the numbers in the text match the tables, turning a paper into slides.

The agent has limits, too. First, the agent makes mistakes, and it makes them in fluent, confident prose. It may misread a variable, drop observations without telling you, or hand you a paraphrase in quotation marks. Second, it doesn’t know the judgment calls of your field: which cases belong in the sample, or what a concept means in your literature. Third, what it reads is sent to the company’s servers, so confidential material needs care.

Each of these problems has a routine that handles it, and you already know most of them from supervising research assistants and refereeing papers. Part 3 is about those routines.

What do you need?

  • A paid Claude plan. Claude Code needs Claude Pro or higher (about $20 a month, cancel any time; see pricing). A university Claude Team or Enterprise plan also works.
  • A computer with Windows 10 or 11, macOS, or Linux (Ubuntu or Debian).
  • Permission to install software. A university laptop may need IT approval. Try first, and ask if you are blocked.

How is the course organized?

The course has four parts. The first three are hands-on and take about an hour each. The fourth is a live demonstration, followed by homework. If something fails along the way, nothing is broken: check the troubleshooting table of that part, or ask the agent what went wrong.

1

Steps 1.1–1.5 · about an hour

Setup

Install the app, make a project folder, meet the agent, let it set up Python, and install a reader for the files it writes.

You end with a working agent in a folder of your own.

2

Steps 2.1–2.5 · about an hour

First Steps

Put the agent to work on what you know: a Word draft, a pile of readings, a small dataset. Along the way, give it standing instructions and two skills.

You end with a routine for Word documents, a table of readings you verified, and one analysis whose numbers you checked yourself.

3

Steps 3.1–3.4 · about an hour

Staying in Charge

Trust, verification and control. Keep the agent in its folder, lock the raw data, see what it did, build checks into the work, and have it reviewed by a second pair of eyes.

You end with work you can put your name on, because you can trace it.

4

Live demo · then homework

A Paper in 50 Minutes

We give the agent a single prompt and watch it produce a short empirical paper and a slide deck. Then comes your part: go home and study what it left behind, from the paper and the deck to the decision log, the code and the skills it used.

You end with a view of a whole project, and practice in reading an agent’s work as a referee would.

A short glossary

  • Agent: an AI model that acts on your computer (reads files, runs code, saves results) in a loop until a task is done.
  • Prompt: the instruction you type for the agent. In this course, prompts to copy come in boxes.
  • Session: one conversation with the agent. Each session starts fresh.
  • Context window: what the model can see at once. Long sessions fill it up, and quality drops.
  • Permission mode: how much the agent may do without asking you. We start with Manual: it asks every time.
  • Markdown (.md): plain text with a few formatting symbols. It is the common language of people and agents.
  • AGENTS.md: your standing instructions, which the agent reads at the start of every session.
  • Skill: a recipe file the agent follows for one type of task.
  • Terminal: a text window for typing commands. The agent uses one behind the scenes. You don’t have to.
  • Environment (Python): a project’s own copy of Python and its add-ons, kept in the .venv folder.
  • Harness: a word you will hear later. It is the program around the model that gives it these abilities. Claude Code is one.

Useful links

Many people have written about agents for researchers. These are the ones we find most useful for this audience.

For people who don’t code

  • Chris Blattman, AI for Professionals Who Don’t Code. A political economist’s guide to running projects, meetings and everyday academic work with an agent.
  • Mushtaq Bilal, A Beginner’s Guide to Claude Code for (Non-Technical) Academics. Written for the humanities and social sciences. The link is a summary; the original is a long post on X.
  • Carl Vellotti, Claude Code for Researchers. Interview transcripts, PDFs and field notes, with good advice on verifying quotes.
  • Neal Caren, social-data-analysis. Ready-made skills for qualitative and quantitative sociological research, from interview analysis to literature synthesis.

If you work with data

  • Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham, Claude Code for Applied Economists. Eight video episodes, from getting started to verification.
  • Scott Cunningham, Claude Code Changed How I Work. A long series on empirical research with an agent. Some posts are for subscribers.
  • Mihail Velikov, AI in Business & Economic Research. One-page summaries of more than 180 guides and papers.

Background reading

  • Ethan Mollick, One Useful Thing. The best running commentary on working with AI.
  • Our own list: suggested readings and resources.
